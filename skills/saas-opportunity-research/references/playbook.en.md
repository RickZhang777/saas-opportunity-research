# Research Web and SaaS opportunities

**English** · [简体中文](playbook.zh-CN.md)

<a id="screen"></a>
## 1. Decide which products deserve time

Record the audience and country, the founder's time and resources, the kinds of products in scope, and the endpoint requested. Age, target traffic, and shortlist size are user choices. For an early-follower search, a useful starting profile is a browser product from a comparable operator, one recognizable customer job, a recent launch, and two comparable months with a positive change. Prefer a third month when available. Set age and exclusion rules with the user when they materially affect the decision.

Search by buyer, job, and output. For example, search for "bookkeeper reconcile bank CSV" alongside new listings, category rankings, and public payment profiles. [Toolify](https://www.toolify.ai/), [Traffic.cv](https://traffic.cv/), [There's An AI For That](https://theresanaiforthat.com/), and [TrustMRR](https://trustmrr.com/) are optional starting points. Use accessible public or authorized sources. Prioritize official product pages, dated customer accounts, and the original evidence behind a listing. Record an inaccessible source and how it was attempted. Continue with independent available work.

Deduplicate by product ID, canonical domain, and documented aliases. Keep redirects, brand moves, and product launches separate. A product listing is one discovery event. Verify operator comparability through dated founder or team evidence. A named founder, revenue listing, or simple site is insufficient to establish a solo operation.

If using leaderboards, record the source, category, period, ranking metric, visible range, and board length. A page's last rows may be the middle of the full ranking. A rank in an absolute-growth board tells you about change, regardless of total scale. Compare ranks only on the same basis and record first appearance separately. Use established leaders as useful alternatives where relevant.

<a id="evidence"></a>
## 2. Build the evidence for each finalist

A practical deep dive compares 3–5 direct competitors or close alternatives if available. A free or manual workflow can be the strongest substitute. For each material claim, record the source URL, collection date, period, scope, metric, and limitation.

| Topic | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Product and buyer | User and payer, purchase trigger and repeat unit, input → processing → output. Separate official claims, demonstrations, and your own trials. Check whether the buyer is already spending money, time, or rework on the job. |
| Growth | Latest 2–3 complete months where available. Compare the same domain, source, metric, device, and geography. Report base, change, and rate. Keep missing, zero, stale, blocked, and conflicting values distinct. A zero base gives an undefined growth rate. More than one increase is needed to infer acceleration. Label estimates and small samples. |
| Traffic quality | Identify countries, channels, and the landing pages tied to the paid task where data is available. Brand navigation, free tools, and paid acquisition can explain growth. A second provider using the same upstream data provides limited corroboration. |
| Age and operator | Separate domain registration, earliest dated product record, company formation, directory listing, and actual launch. Record founder claims and any missing support, outsourcing, or distribution dependencies. |
| Pricing and payment | Verify official currency, billable period, annual total, quotas, extras, and free allowances. Distinguish a recommended badge or default selection from observed sales. Separate public payment-provider disclosures, payment-data verification, and a checkout actually observed. Stop at the authorized boundary. |
| Buyer behavior | Look for dated descriptions of a recent task, paid alternatives, adoption, repeat use, and complaints. Record speaker and sample limits. Revenue verified by a platform is a defined snapshot. Keep net profit, refunds, retention, MRR, and collected revenue separate. |
| Competition | Compare the same buyer, trigger, and accepted result. A low-cost competitor, an integrated feature, and a spreadsheet may be more relevant than the biggest product in the category. |
| Delivery and economics | Define one acceptable deliverable. Identify upstream dependencies, permission to ship the output or redistribute assets, retries, manual QA, support, and failure recovery. For contribution before acquisition, subtract variable processing, retries, storage, fees, refund allowance, and required labor from realized price. Use sourced values, explicit scenarios, or unknowns. |

Avoid calculating conversions from unmatched traffic and payment windows. Two observations can support a rising or falling interval. Compare at least two interval growth rates for acceleration, with compatible bases and periods.

Label every decision-relevant evidence item with its **subject** (competitor, current alternative, proposed offer) and **level** (hypothesis, desktop signal, observed behavior, transaction). A documented transaction for a competitor belongs in that competitor's column. A buyer using the output from your own offer belongs in the proposed offer's observed-behavior column. Keep founder self-report, independent review, provider estimates, and your own observations identifiable within those labels.

<a id="decision"></a>
## 3. Turn evidence into a decision

Write one sentence with a **payer, trigger, and result they can accept**. Describe the buyer's existing workflow, one measurable improvement, and a channel that can reach people doing that job today. If a segment already has the feature, compare the whole task with the same input and measure the part you expect to improve. A change in branding, language, packaging, or price needs a buyer-side reason to matter.

- **Validate first** when a specific and reachable buyer has credible evidence of costly behavior or payment and the result can be tested at low cost. Record any open decision-critical gaps.
- **Watch** when the pain looks plausible but payment, acquisition, fit, or delivery is uncertain.
- **Defer** when a strong substitute, non-replicable asset, delivery burden, or scope mismatch dominates.

Name the strongest counterexample. For a hypothesis based on a competitor, identify what might make that competitor's results hard to repeat. Recommend one experiment with the largest unknown, buyer criteria and channel, deliverable, ask or price, time and cash limits, and pass/continue/stop criteria. A small test guides the next decision. If recruitment fails, record the reachability gap separately from buyers declining an actual offer. If adoption requires expensive manual help, include that time before projecting margin.

When authorized, test with a real buyer's eligible sample and compare to their normal method. Track actual use and payment separately from praise. A testable scope and acceptance criteria can follow when there is enough behavior evidence for the user's decision.

<a id="review"></a>
## 4. Deliver and maintain the research

Use the shortest report that supports the decision. An audit table can use **Product | Buyer | Job and result | Price | Growth | Payment evidence | Age/operator | Alternatives | Recommendation**. Put detail and citations in an appendix. Count researched products and qualified opportunities separately.

If maintaining a library, keep one product record per stable ID and separate opportunity records keyed by buyer, trigger, and accepted result. Store dated evidence and status changes. Keep the user's decisions and analyst recommendations in different fields. Include a short run review with planned and completed scope, attempted path, missing data or blocked step, impact, and next action. List incomplete work accurately. A saved file or structural check verifies delivery and consistency only.

For a weekly review, compare directions as well as sites. Explain what changed, preserve prior decisions, and give one next experiment and an optional backup if useful. A handoff should include the evidence index, unknowns, strongest objection, the customer's switching reason, scope, acceptance criteria, and current authorization.

<a id="example"></a>
## Worked example with fictional data

Two websites both turn interview recordings into transcripts. The research question is whether editors at small oral-history organizations would pay for **a source-linked quote sheet for each interview**.

| Signal | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Candidate A's same-source estimated visits are 420 → 950 → 1,280 across three complete months | Two increases, +530 then +330. The latest absolute gain and growth rate are lower. These are desktop signals for the competitor. |
| Candidate B's estimate is 0 → 90 → 260 and its paid plan is $12/month | The first rate is undefined, and the provider's zero may indicate limited coverage. There is one comparable increase after a positive base. The price is a listed offer. |
| A has provider-verified revenue of $230 in the last 30 days. The buyer mix and monthly profit are unknown. | Competitor transaction evidence. The period and metric differ from the visit estimates. The proposed offer has no transaction evidence. |
| An editor says they already use a free transcript and manually check names and timecodes | If the account is dated and tied to a real recent task, it supports the existing-workflow pain. Ask to see the process or result and measure rework. |

**Decision: watch.** The existing transcript plus manual review may already be good enough. The first experiment would recruit five editors with a recent eligible recording through public oral-history groups, show a source-linked quote sheet made from a consented sample, and offer a $15 one-time delivery. Hypothetical cap: two days and $30 excluding labor. Continue if two independent editors pay, use the result, and review time stays inside the agreed limit. Record contact failure separately. If they decline after seeing the deliverable and price, use their reason to revise the offer or stop. These are proposed thresholds, not observations.
